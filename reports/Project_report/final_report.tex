\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\label{abstract}
Coordination in multi-agent language-model systems is difficult to assess: a successful group outcome does not show how agents’ actions relate, and shared feedback can conceal individual contributions. Tool-use studies can make actions and task-state changes visible, but explicit traces alone do not establish coordination. The study asked how single-agent, independent multi-agent, and coordination-oriented policies differed in completion, action use, and stopping behavior in a controlled tool-use task. An initial repeated guessing-game implementation was followed by a synthetic bug-fix experiment using three model-backed policies, 16 fixture variants, and four repetitions per policy-fixture combination. In total, 192 completed episodes were examined through tool-event logs, outcome summaries, and categorical action-profile analysis; a later replay check tested recorded guessing-game transitions. Full success was recorded in 187 episodes (97.4\%); five were partial, and none failed. Success rates were 100\% for the single-agent policy, 93.75\% for independent multi-agent, and 98.44\% for coordination-oriented policy, while all five partial episodes involved premature finalization on billing fixtures despite remaining call budget. The action-profile estimates showed small associations with outcomes but remained exploratory because they were based on sparse categories. The results show that explicit action traces support auditing and descriptive comparisons in this synthetic setting, but do not establish policy superiority or causal coordination. The study contributes an auditable tool-use framework and identifies participation and stopping behavior as candidates for future validation; it does not replicate information-theoretic findings about emergence.

\section{Background and motivation}
As artificial intelligence moves beyond single prompt-response chatbots toward systems of interacting language-model agents, developers increasingly assign multiple agents to complex workflows. This raises a basic scientific question: do these systems behave as integrated collectives with higher-order synergy, or as loosely connected agents reacting independently to shared feedback? Christoph Riedl’s paper, \emph{Emergent Coordination in Multi-Agent Language Models}, addresses this gap by asking whether a group’s changing states contain predictive information about its future that cannot be attributed to any one agent alone (Riedl, 2026, pp. 1–2).

To investigate this, the paper uses information-theoretic measures, particularly time-delayed mutual information (TDMI) and partial information decomposition (PID). TDMI evaluates predictive relationships across rounds; PID separates information available uniquely from individual sources from redundancy shared across sources and synergy that is available only when sources are considered together (Riedl, 2026, pp. 3–5; Williams and Beer, 2010). These measures describe structure in the agents’ behaviour. They do not imply human-like understanding, establish causation, or by themselves show that a group performs better.

The experiments use a minimalist group-guessing game. Agents make private guesses toward a shared hidden target and receive the same directional feedback after each round, without direct agent-to-agent communication. Three prompt conditions are compared: \textbf{Plain}, which gives standard task instructions; \textbf{Persona}, which gives agents distinct backgrounds and identities; and \textbf{Theory of Mind (ToM)}, which asks agents to anticipate peers’ likely guesses and account for them when choosing their own (Riedl, 2026, pp. 2–3). The paper reports evidence of dynamical emergence across conditions and stronger task alignment and identity-linked differentiation under ToM. However, overall success did not differ significantly across the prompt conditions, so the findings do not establish that synergy automatically improves task performance (Riedl, 2026, pp. 6–9).

This topic matters as multi-agent systems are applied to software engineering, scientific discovery and business automation. The paper’s findings motivate three practical considerations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Challenge the assumption that more agents create synergy.} Adding agents does not guarantee performance beyond that of individuals. Redundancy and synergy need to be considered together, and their presence should be measured rather than inferred from group size or a successful final answer. These terms are defined in Section 1.2.
\item \textbf{Diagnose uncoordinated behaviour.} Shared feedback can lead agents to make similar corrections without useful coordination. Information-theoretic measures offer ways to inspect predictive structure and distinguish some forms of shared or complementary behaviour; they are diagnostic tools, not guarantees against oscillation, reasoning loops or other deployment failures.
\item \textbf{Treat prompts as design choices to evaluate.} Persona and ToM instructions are architectural levers that can change how agents contribute. The paper finds stronger alignment and differentiation under ToM, but does not show that prompt engineering alone reliably produces higher-performing teams across tasks.
\end{enumerate}

The paper was selected for its depth: it connects ideas about emergence and collective intelligence with measurements of how agents’ behaviour relates over time (Rosas et al., 2020; Riedl et al., 2021). The project guide noted the complexity of reproducing the full information-theoretic analysis and advised narrowing the scope. Accordingly, the experiments in this thesis are presented as related project work, not as a replication of the paper’s claims (Section 1.6).

\section{Key terms}
This section defines key terms used in the the reference papers and discusses our plain-language explanations to help with understanding.
\begin{description}
\item[Mutual information (MI).]
\textbf{Definition:} MI quantifies how much information one variable provides about another, expressed in bits; it is zero when the variables are independent (Newman et al., 2022).
To put it simply, MI tells us how much knowing one thing helps us predict another. Imagine recording two coin flips. If the flips tend to match, knowing the first gives us a clue about the second, so they have MI. If the flips are independent, knowing the first tells us nothing about the second, and their MI is zero. MI describes this statistical relationship; by itself, it does not show that one flip caused the other.

\item[Time-delayed mutual information (TDMI).]
\textbf{Definition:} MI is calculated between a system’s state at time \(t\) and a state at a later time \(t+\ell\). Riedl mainly uses the next-round lag, \(\ell=1\) (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–5; Luppi et al., 2022).
It asks whether knowing something now helps predict something later. For example, today's traffic level may help predict tomorrow's traffic level. This is a link across time; it does not, by itself, show that today's traffic caused tomorrow's.

\item[Dynamical emergence (emergence capacity).]
\textbf{Definition:} For each agent pair, the paper measures the PID synergy between the agents’ current states and their joint future state, then summarizes across pairs. This operational test asks whether the pair’s future is predictable from the two current states together in a way not supplied by either alone (Riedl, 2026, pp. 2, 4–5; Rosas et al., 2020).
This asks whether two pieces of information together tell us something about the future that neither tells us alone. Imagine an alarm that sounds when exactly one of two switches is on: seeing either switch by itself does not tell us whether the alarm will sound, but seeing both does. In the paper's test, the two pieces are agents' current states and the future is their later states. Finding this joint predictive information does not, by itself, show that the agents caused each other's actions or performed better.

\item[Partial information decomposition (PID).]
\textbf{Definition:} For two sources and a target, PID partitions their information about the target into source-specific, shared, and joint-only components (Williams and Beer, 2010).
Think of the two sources as two clues and the target as the answer we want to work out. PID separates information provided by just one clue, information both clues provide, and information that becomes available only when the clues are combined:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unique information} is information supplied by one clue but not the other. For example, one note might contain a detail that is absent from a second note.
\item \textbf{Redundancy} is information repeated in both clues. For example, two thermometers might report the same room temperature.
\item \textbf{Synergy} is information available only by combining the clues. For example, an alarm might sound when exactly one of two switches is on: knowing either switch alone does not tell us whether it will sound, but knowing both does.
\end{itemize}
PID is not uniquely defined: formulations differ in how they quantify the components and may return different values for the same data (Kolchinsky, 2022; Newman et al., 2022; Varley, 2024). Riedl uses Williams–Beer \(I_{\min}\) and checks robustness with MMI, which typically yields more conservative synergy estimates (Riedl, 2026, p. 5). Neither redundancy nor synergy is a performance score; shared information is task-relevant only if it concerns the task outcome.

\item[O-information.]
\textbf{Definition:} O-information is a multivariate measure that summarizes whether a set of variables is more redundancy-dominated or synergy-dominated (Stramaglia et al., 2021). Positive values indicate redundancy dominance; negative values indicate synergy dominance.
It gives an overall summary of whether information across a set is mostly repeated or mostly depends on combining variables. For example, several sensors that all report the same temperature provide overlapping information; separate clues that only make sense together illustrate information gained through combination. Unlike PID, O-information does not break the information about one target into contributions from particular sources.

\item[Macro signal and goal alignment.]
\textbf{Definition:} The paper’s practical alignment test uses a group-level macro signal (the group error) and asks whether the agents’ current states jointly carry information about its future value. Its triplet measure, \(I_3\), is the MI between three agents’ current states and the next macro signal (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–5).
A macro signal is one value that summarizes a group's state, such as a team's total score. Goal alignment asks whether the group's current states help predict a task-relevant future, such as whether that score will move closer to a goal. If everyone makes the same move only because they received the same alert, that similarity alone does not show that their actions are helping the group reach its goal.

\item[Agent differentiation.]
\textbf{Definition:} Riedl tests whether agents show consistent person-specific patterns using hierarchical models that separate a shared time effect from agent-specific levels and time trends (Riedl, 2026, pp. 5–6).
It asks whether people or agents show lasting differences after accounting for changes that affect everyone. For example, a whole team may complete more work on a quiet day, while one team member still consistently completes more than others on comparable days.

\item[Identity-locked differentiation.]
\textbf{Definition:} This is agent-specific differentiation that remains associated with the same identity after accounting for shared temporal dynamics (Riedl, 2026, p. 6).
The difference stays associated with the same person over time, rather than appearing only because everyone is responding to the same situation. For example, one participant may consistently respond faster than others, even as the task becomes easier or harder for everyone.

\item[Dynamic alignment.]
\textbf{Definition:} The paper distinguishes cross-agent temporal coordination from identity-locked effects using time-shifted null tests; examples it discusses include mutual adaptation, turn-taking, and oscillation (Riedl, 2026, p. 6 and Appendix A.4).
It asks whether the relationship between people's actions changes over time, rather than reflecting only their usual individual differences. For example, in a discussion, one person might regularly respond to the previous speaker by offering an alternative, creating a back-and-forth pattern. If both people change their behaviour because of the same event, that alone does not show they are responding to each other.

\item[Complementarity.]
\textbf{Definition:} Complementarity refers to distinct contributions that fit together in service of a shared goal; the paper contrasts it with redundancy, or alignment on shared goals (Riedl, 2026, pp. 3, 7–10).
Different contributions are complementary when they fit together to help achieve the same goal. For example, one person might gather information while another checks it. Simply doing different things is not enough; the contributions must work together and matter to the task. This does not require sophisticated reasoning or consciousness.
\end{description}

\section{The guessing task}
Each agent privately picks a whole number between 0 and 50. The guesses are added, and the sum is compared with a hidden target. Agents cannot see each other’s guesses. They are not told how many agents are in the group. After every round, all of them receive the same feedback, “too high” or “too low”, and the game ends when the sum equals the target (Riedl, 2026, pp. 3, 16; Goldstone et al., 2024).

The main runs used groups of 10 GPT-4.1 agents at temperature 1, with 200 groups in each condition and 600 in total. An earlier sweep of 7,150 groups, with sizes from 3 to 15 and temperatures from 0 to 1, showed that each extra member lowered the odds of success by about 8% (Riedl, 2026, p. 6).

The feedback is about the group, never about one agent. “Too high” tells an agent that the total is off, but not who pushed it there. If every agent corrects in the same direction, the group overshoots together. If the corrections are spread out, one agent’s move can balance another’s. Each round leaves a record of individual guesses, group movement and distance from the target, and that record is enough to measure coordination without any direct messages between agents. Common feedback can also make agents move in lockstep with no real teamwork, which is why the paper does not treat success as its measure of coordination (Riedl, 2026, pp. 3–5).

\section{Prompt conditions and research questions}
Three prompt conditions are compared. \textbf{Plain} gives only the task. \textbf{Persona} gives each agent a character with a background, skills, personality and values. \textbf{Theory of mind (ToM)} adds one more instruction: think about what the other agents are likely to do and how that changes the group result (Riedl, 2026, pp. 2–3). The interest is in how prompts change the way agents coordinate. Overall success, in fact, did not differ significantly across the three conditions (Riedl, 2026, p. 6).

The paper is built around three questions (Riedl, 2026, pp. 2–3):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Do multi-agent LLM systems possess the capacity for emergence?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} What functional advantages, such as synergistic coordination and higher performance, arise when emergence occurs?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Can prompts, roles, and reasoning structures steer coordination toward outcome-relevant, goal-directed synergy?
\end{enumerate}
These are the \textbf{motivating paper’s questions}. The Phase 2 experiment in this project cannot answer them, since its task, policy conditions and analyses are all different. The project’s own questions were written after the final-run results had been examined. They are descriptive questions and were not set in advance as hypotheses:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Project RQ1 --- Completion:} In the synthetic bug-fix environment, how do the single-agent, independent multi-agent and prompted-coordination policies differ in full completion, partial completion and failure across the matched fixture assignments?
\item \textbf{Project RQ2 --- Action use:} How do these policies differ in the number of tool calls, the mix of tools used and, for the two multi-agent conditions, the split of actions between agents?
\item \textbf{Project RQ3 --- Stopping behaviour:} How do these policies differ in the number of tool calls made before finalisation, and in premature finalisation, defined as ending an episode while a required fixture defect is still unresolved?
\end{enumerate}
The Phase 2 outcomes and traces measure these quantities directly. With 192 episodes, one model (gpt-5-mini) and 16 synthetic fixture variants, they support comparisons inside this setting only. They do not give causal effects, and they say nothing about the paper’s questions on emergence, synergy or general-purpose coordination. Section 10.7 answers each project question in turn.

\section{Analysis methods in the paper}
Riedl combines four tests with a set of null checks (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–6). Before any test is run, each guess is turned into an \textbf{equal-share deviation}: the guess minus target\(/N\), where \(N\) is the group size. With a target of 50 and 10 agents, a guess of 7 becomes \(+2\). This puts all agents on one scale, so the remaining variation shows who is making up for whom (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–5).

\begin{description}
\item[Emergence capacity.] For each pair of agents, the test asks whether their two current states, read together, predict the pair’s joint state one round later. PID splits this predictive information and the synergy part is kept. The median across all pairs is the group score. Only pairwise (order-two) synergy is captured, but no group-level signal has to be defined (Riedl, 2026, p. 4; Luppi et al., 2022; Williams and Beer, 2010). It shows predictive structure, which on its own is not causal evidence.
\item[Practical criterion.] Here a group-level \textbf{macro signal} is used: the group error, or the sum of guesses minus the target. The test checks whether this signal predicts its own next value better than all individual agents added together. A positive score points to emergence. Redundancy across agents pulls the score down, so it can turn negative even when synergy is present (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–5).
\item[Coalition test.] \(I_3\) is how much a group of three agents jointly tells about the next macro signal. \(G_3 = I_3 - \max I_2\) is what the triplet adds over its most informative pair. When \(G_3 > 0\), no pair can explain what the three agents together reveal about the goal (Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–5).
\item[Agent differentiation.] Three nested hierarchical models are fitted to the deviations. The first has only a shared time effect, the second adds a separate level for each agent, and the third adds a separate time slope for each agent. Likelihood-ratio tests then ask whether agents differ in level and in rate of change. Because the shared time effect absorbs group-wide swings, the agent effects reflect identity rather than common feedback. A difference between agents still does not amount to synergy (Riedl, 2026, pp. 5–6).
\end{description}

The \textbf{falsification and null tests} look for other explanations. Row-wise shuffles break the link between an agent and its contributions. Time-shift (column) shuffles keep each agent’s own dynamics but break the timing across agents. P-values from separate groups are combined with Fisher’s method. Detrending, a baseline with no cross-agent synergy, and alternative estimators (a Jeffreys prior and Miller–Madow correction) check for shared trends, autocorrelation and small-sample bias (Riedl, 2026, pp. 5–6; Appendix A.4). These tests make the claims open to falsification, though no single shuffle can prove a mechanism.

Other higher-order measures, such as dynamical O-information, address related but separate questions and are not a drop-in replacement for PID (Stramaglia et al., 2021; Varley, 2026).

\section{Paper findings, limits and project scope}
In the main experiments, dynamical emergence shows up in all three conditions. ToM stands out. It produces the most stable task alignment and the clearest identity-linked differentiation of the three. Personas on their own make agents behave differently, while the added “think about others” instruction gives the strongest sign of goal-directed complementarity. Neither synergy nor redundancy alone predicts performance; their interaction does. The indirect effect of ToM through synergy is only marginal in the main text and should not be read as firm mediation evidence (Riedl, 2026, pp. 7–9).

The robustness runs with Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B, Gemini 2.0 Flash and Qwen3 give mixed results. The authors list several limits:
\begin{itemize}
\item a single, deliberately minimal task;
\item short, discrete trajectories, where information estimates shift with the choice of estimator and binning (the main analysis uses only two quantile bins);
\item some analyses capped at pairwise, order-two synergy; and
\item early stopping, which ties task success to the time a group had for coordination.
\end{itemize}
Synergy and redundancy also depend on each other, which makes a clean link to performance hard to draw. The authors call for work across more tasks and measures (Riedl, 2026, pp. 5, 9–10). These conclusions describe the paper’s own experiments and are not a general law.

This project’s phases are separate from the paper’s findings. Phase 1 adapts the guessing game, but there is no evidence that it reproduces the full set of interventions or analyses. In line with the guide’s concern about complexity, Phase 2 does not attempt the paper’s time-delayed PID, macro-signal tests or differentiation models (coordination\allowbreak\_analysis.py; Riedl, 2026, pp. 4–6). It moves to synthetic bug fixing instead. Software debugging is the author’s everyday professional work, which made it a natural setting in which to watch agents divide a job. Every tool call and every fixture outcome is logged, and the analysis relates categorical action profiles to episode outcomes. The final run has 192 completed episodes: 187 successes, 5 partials and no failures. The 56 retry records are not counted as extra episodes (aggregate\allowbreak\_summary.json). Studies of synergy inside a single LLM work at a different level, looking at how information is organised within one model rather than between agents (Urbina-Rodriguez et al., 2026).

The tool-use setting gives tighter control. Each action, state change and outcome can be inspected after the run. It does not, however, test whether the paper’s conclusions carry over to other tasks, and it does not reproduce the paper’s emergence measures.

\section{How Phase 1 developed}
Phase 1 used a repeated group-guessing task. Each agent supplied a number, the engine combined the guesses, and all agents received the same directional feedback. The prompts were later adjusted so that each agent could use its own earlier guesses without seeing the guesses made by its peers. A persona version came next. It added character descriptions and an instruction to think about the other agents at the same time, so the effect of either prompt change on its own cannot be measured. The project therefore carried forward a private-guess, shared-feedback setup into the later bridge and tool-use work.

\section{Game rules}
The Phase 1 engine (\texttt{experiment.py}) supports two ways to combine guesses. In \textbf{sum} mode, each agent chooses an integer from 0 to 50. Python’s \texttt{random.randint} samples the hidden integer target from 51 to 100, and the guesses are added before comparison. In \textbf{mean} mode, the default ranges for both the target and guesses are 0 to 100. The engine takes the arithmetic mean of the guesses and applies Python’s \texttt{round} before comparing it with the integer target. The run configuration records the mode, ranges, target, temperature and round limit. These are the rules implemented in this repository; they do not show that the full protocol or set of treatments from the paper was reproduced.

The agents receive one of three labels: \texttt{CORRECT!}, \texttt{too HIGH}, or \texttt{too LOW}. They do not see how far the group value is from the target. A correct guess ends the game; otherwise, another round is played up to the configured limit, which is normally 20 rounds in the runner settings. The target is printed in the local console for the experiment operator, but is left out of the agents’ prompts.

For each round, the game manager saves a \texttt{Round} record with guesses indexed by agent, the group result, feedback, target, mode, prompts and response identifiers. Parsing failures and API fallbacks can also be marked. The field names need care: \texttt{Round.average} holds the sum in sum mode, but the unrounded mean in mean mode. \texttt{rounded\allowbreak\_average} holds the value used for comparison—the sum or the rounded mean. The mode must be checked before either field is interpreted.

\section{What agents see}
The agent receives the shared round history, but \texttt{\allowbreak\_build\allowbreak\_strategic\allowbreak\_prompt} pulls out only \texttt{round\allowbreak\_data.guesses[self.agent\allowbreak\_id]} from each earlier round. That guess is paired with the group’s feedback label. The prompt describes the game and allowed range, then lists that agent’s own guesses and the feedback before asking for another integer. In the first round, it says that the agent has not guessed yet and that no group feedback is available.

No peer guesses, peer identities or direct messages are included in this prompt. Agents can respond to the same group result, but each prompt keeps a separate record of their guesses. That shared signal is limited: it shows that the group is high or low without revealing which agent caused it. The initial guess is generated by the model as well. Prompts and round records were saved for selected inspection.

\section{Calls and response recovery}
Agents are queried concurrently within a round; the rounds themselves run one after another. Responses do not always contain a clean integer. The API client may return a marked numeric fallback. If parsing fails, the agent can reuse its last successful guess, or use the midpoint of the allowed range if no earlier guess is available. The parser also tries several extraction strategies, and some paths select or clamp a number from the response. A guess that falls within the allowed range is therefore not necessarily the number the model returned unchanged. As the code developed, more of the parsing and fallback behaviour was recorded with the round data.

\section{The synthetic bug-fix task}
Synthetic bug fixing was chosen because each task can be defined in advance, actions can be limited, and the environment can check the result against fixture rules. The fixture builder creates eight templates with two variants each, or 16 variants in total. Some variants add a third required defect. Agents use five tools: \texttt{retrieve\allowbreak\_file}, \texttt{search\allowbreak\_symbol}, \texttt{run\allowbreak\_tests}, \texttt{apply\allowbreak\_patch} and \texttt{finalize\allowbreak\_ticket} (baseline/plan.md; baseline/tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_environment.py; baseline/settings.py).

Each fixture lists its files, symbols, defects, requirements and checks. The environment checks each tool call against its argument format and current state. Retrieval and symbol search reveal fixture information; an accepted patch changes the simulated state; a test call checks the declared conditions; finalization applies the outcome rules. Episodes are labelled \texttt{success}, \texttt{partial} or \texttt{failure}. Calls that do not fit the current state or step budget are recorded as invalid. The task therefore records a sequence of actions and state changes instead of one shared numerical signal.

This design sits alongside work that studies tool use and multi-agent systems. ReAct combines reasoning with actions in external environments, and API-Bank evaluates planning, retrieval and API calls (Yao et al., 2023; Li, M. et al., 2023). AutoGen and CAMEL study configurable or role-based conversations between language-model agents (Wu et al., 2023; Li, G. et al., 2023). The LLM-Coordination benchmark focuses on decisions in coordination games (Agashe et al., 2023). Surveys cover collaboration methods and the move from single calls to multi-tool workflows with intermediate state and feedback (Tran et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Here, actions come from a fixture-specific allow-list, state transitions are deterministic, and agents take turns using shared context. The cited work provides background; it does not make this task equivalent to those systems or support a novelty claim.

The evaluator is described in Section 5.1. Its fixture checks are not the same as validating a repair in a real repository.

The event sequence separates information gathering, patching, testing and finalization. It also records accepted patches and redundant, invalid or over-budget calls. This makes the run easier to inspect; it does not show that tool use is inherently more coordinated or capable.

\section{Policies and action categories}
Scripted policies were used first to test the environment and analysis without involving model responses. The four conditions were \texttt{single\allowbreak\_agent}, \texttt{random\allowbreak\_policy}, \texttt{independent\allowbreak\_multi\allowbreak\_agent} and \texttt{prompted\allowbreak\_coordination}. They represent a one-agent reference, random selection from valid actions, separate multi-agent plans and a shared scripted plan. In this pilot, “prompted coordination” names the scripted plan; no LLM prompt intervention was involved. All policies act in sequence against shared state, unlike Phase 1, where agents respond concurrently within each round (baseline/tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_baselines.py).

Model-backed versions were later added: \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_single\allowbreak\_agent}, \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_independent\allowbreak\_multi\allowbreak\_agent} and \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_prompted\allowbreak\_coordination}. Their adapter requests a structured action and records parsing attempts, errors and fallbacks. These conditions compare one-agent and multi-agent workflows, as well as independent and coordination-oriented instructions. They do not isolate every cause of a difference or reproduce the paper’s Plain, Persona and theory-of-mind conditions. They also rely on an external model service. Persona studies were outside the initial tool-use scope (baseline/plan.md; baseline/tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_baselines.py; baseline/llm\allowbreak\_client.py).

Raw traces preserve detail, but can create many distinct categories for analysis. The event types were therefore grouped as \texttt{retrieve}, \texttt{verify}, \texttt{update}, \texttt{finalize} and \texttt{error\allowbreak\_or\allowbreak\_noop}. The analysis computes capped per-agent counts and a \texttt{coarsened\allowbreak\_action\allowbreak\_profile}, while retaining other representations for comparison. In the scripted pilot, the profile produced fewer joint states than the full traces. This makes the data easier to handle, but does not show that the labels capture how a person would interpret each trajectory. The planned human labelling and inter-rater checks were not completed (baseline/coordination\allowbreak\_analysis.py; baseline/validation.md).

\section{From event logs to outcomes}
The planned trajectory record included event order, actor, tool, arguments, outputs, task progress, invalid-call details and episode outcome. The implemented pipeline saves an \texttt{episode.jsonl} event stream, \texttt{summary.json} and \texttt{task\allowbreak\_fixture.json}. Sweep runs also save configuration, progress, aggregate summaries and a manifest. Together these files link a fixture and policy to its actions and final classification. The primary planned measure was categorical mutual information between two agents’ action representations and the three-way episode outcome, written as \$I(A\allowbreak\_1,A\allowbreak\_2;Y)\$. The implemented report uses joint action profiles from eligible two-agent episodes. Single-agent episodes are shown separately and are not included in this estimate. Smoothing checks, bootstrap intervals and permutation tests provide additional diagnostics. The measure links action categories to outcome labels, but remains an association between compressed categorical summaries. Small samples, sparse joint states or outcomes that rarely vary can make it unstable or uninformative. A non-zero estimate does not show that a policy caused success or that a collective property emerged.

This is not the paper’s temporal mutual information or partial information decomposition. It does not measure paper-style synergy, redundancy, differentiation or complementarity. It is an action-to-outcome analysis for a different task, not a replication of the paper’s findings.

\section{Task setup and fixture catalog}
Phase 2 runs on synthetic bug-fix fixtures, not a live repository. The function \texttt{build\allowbreak\_bugfix\allowbreak\_task\allowbreak\_fixtures()} in \texttt{tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_environment.py} builds eight task templates with two variants each, for 16 fixtures. Each fixture includes a title and description, in-memory source and placeholder test files, symbol references, recognised patch options, test targets and required defect IDs. The two harder variants add a third defect. Each patch pairs exact old text with its replacement and the defect it fixes. An arbitrary patch is not accepted as a successful repair.

Runtime settings are kept in \texttt{settings.py}: environment and policy names, agent count, call budget, repetitions or seeds, batch size, output prefix and LLM defaults. The thesis configuration has three model-backed policies, two agents in each multi-agent condition, a 12-call limit, four repetitions per fixture, seeds starting at 200 and strict LLM handling. Before running episodes, \texttt{run\allowbreak\_baseline\allowbreak\_sweep.py} writes the run configuration and manifest. The single-agent condition uses one agent regardless of the shared multi-agent setting.

\texttt{ToolUseBugfixEnvironment} stores the changing state of an episode. It starts as \texttt{not\allowbreak\_started}, with a copy of the fixture files and empty records for observations, tests, patches, fixed defects and regressions. Valid progress moves it to \texttt{in\allowbreak\_progress}. The terminal state records success, partial completion or failure. Each instance also saves its seed and fixture, event sequence, invalid-call counts, schema and environment versions, and a short hash based on the environment version, fixture, seed, agent count and call budget.

An episode stops when the policy finalizes or uses the call budget. The environment returns \texttt{success} only if all required defects are fixed and every fixture check passes without a disallowed regression. \texttt{partial} means some required work is complete, checks remain incomplete and no catastrophic regression occurred. Other terminal states are \texttt{failure}. Budget exhaustion uses the same outcome rules as finalization. These labels describe the simulated fixture only. The generated test files have placeholder assertions; \texttt{run\allowbreak\_tests} checks defect IDs and regression guards in the environment state. It does not run pytest, execute the patched code or access a real project.

\section{Tools, validation and state changes}
The environment exposes five tools: \texttt{retrieve\allowbreak\_file}, \texttt{search\allowbreak\_symbol}, \texttt{run\allowbreak\_tests}, \texttt{apply\allowbreak\_patch} and \texttt{finalize\allowbreak\_ticket}. \texttt{list\allowbreak\_valid\allowbreak\_actions()} returns the actions allowed in the current state and within the remaining budget. The list includes known paths, symbols and test targets, plus unapplied patches whose old text is still present. Finalization becomes available after an update or test. No action is offered after the episode ends or the budget runs out. The LLM prompt includes this list, and the environment checks each submitted action again.

Each tool has a fixed argument format. Retrieval needs a string \texttt{path} and may include a \texttt{span} with integer \texttt{start} and \texttt{end}. Symbol search needs a string \texttt{name}; tests take no arguments or a string \texttt{test\allowbreak\_target}; patching needs a string \texttt{patch}; finalization needs a non-empty string \texttt{reason}. Extra or missing keys and incorrect types are rejected. The environment then checks that the referenced file, symbol, test target or patch exists and that the patch’s old text matches. A recognised patch is applied once and updates the fixed-defect record. Tests list unresolved defects, and finalization assigns an outcome.

Invalid calls are placed in five categories: \texttt{schema\allowbreak\_invalid} for malformed arguments or unknown tools; \texttt{reference\allowbreak\_invalid} for unknown files, symbols, tests or patch context; \texttt{state\allowbreak\_invalid} for actions that are not allowed yet, such as finalizing before an update or test; \texttt{semantic\allowbreak\_noop} for empty, repeated or non-changing patches; and \texttt{budget\allowbreak\_invalid} for calls after the budget is used. Calls after the episode ends are state-invalid. Each attempt is saved as an event with the \texttt{error\allowbreak\_or\allowbreak\_noop} category, and updates the relevant counter. The summary’s invalid-call rate divides invalid calls by environment steps. It does not include model-response parsing errors that are recovered before an action reaches the environment.

Retrieval and symbol search save observations; patches change simulated files and defect state; tests save results; finalization sets the outcome. Environment tests cover state changes, early finalization, required fixes, budget partials, repeated-patch no-ops and the hardest fixture (\texttt{test\allowbreak\_tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_environment.py}). Policy tests check finalization rules and scripted outcomes (\texttt{test\allowbreak\_tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_baselines.py}).

The final run had zero invalid environment calls and accepted all 403 patch calls. These figures mainly show that submitted actions matched the schemas, allow-list and fixture catalogue. They do \textbf{not} measure the ability to invent a replacement or the quality of an unrestricted code repair: only recognised patches were available, and \texttt{run\allowbreak\_tests} checks fixture state rather than running patched code. Environment validity is measured after response parsing and action filtering. Separately, 96 invalid model responses across 81 episodes were recovered through retries. Strict handling prevented fallback actions, but those response errors did not reach the environment as invalid calls. The figures describe different stages of the workflow.

\section{Policy adapters and turn-taking}
Each policy implements \texttt{next\allowbreak\_action(environment)} and returns a \texttt{PolicyAction} with an agent ID, tool name and arguments. \texttt{run\allowbreak\_policy\allowbreak\_episode()} requests one action at a time, passes it to \texttt{environment.step()} and records the event. This continues until the task ends or the policy returns no action. Agents take turns against shared state; they are not called simultaneously and have no direct messaging channel.

The scripted \texttt{single\allowbreak\_agent} follows a fixed sequence: retrieve the source, inspect the main symbol, apply the main patch, run targeted tests, apply the helper patch, run the full target, and finalize. \texttt{random\allowbreak\_policy} samples a currently valid action and assigns an agent at random. \texttt{independent\allowbreak\_multi\allowbreak\_agent} cycles through separate per-agent plans for retrieval, patching, testing or review, skipping actions made invalid by earlier turns. \texttt{prompted\allowbreak\_coordination} follows the shared plan in \texttt{build\allowbreak\_demo\allowbreak\_actions()}, assigning inspection, patches, tests and finalization. These are all sequential policies. “Prompted” refers to the scripted plan, not an LLM treatment.

The final experiment compares three model-backed adapters: \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_single\allowbreak\_agent}, \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_independent\allowbreak\_multi\allowbreak\_agent} and \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_prompted\allowbreak\_coordination}. \textbf{How to read this comparison.} Prompted coordination changes two things at once: the role instruction becomes coordinator-focused, and the model can choose the actor instead of following fixed alternation. The independent-versus-prompted comparison therefore cannot isolate the effect of wording. Differences in completion, action mix, participation or stopping could reflect the instruction, the freedom to assign actors, or both. Workload differences are particularly tied to this assignment rule. The comparison describes two policies; it is not a causal estimate of coordination prompting. To isolate the prompt, actor selection would need to stay fixed across prompt conditions, or both instruction and assignment would need to be varied in a factorial design. The current dataset has neither control.

\section{Model requests, parsing and audit}
The LLM client reads settings, with environment variables taking precedence. It recognises \texttt{openai/} and \texttt{azureai/} model prefixes, as well as slash-qualified OpenRouter names, though the client builder supports only OpenAI and Azure AI. Azure requests use the configured API version and a normalised endpoint. The thesis configuration uses \texttt{azureai/gpt-5-mini}; credentials are kept in environment variables. Requests use JSON-object mode. GPT-5 and reasoning-model prefixes use \texttt{max\allowbreak\_completion\allowbreak\_tokens}; other models use \texttt{max\allowbreak\_tokens}.

Each prompt includes the task, current progress, the last six actions in shared history, agent IDs and the current valid-action list. Shared instructions require one allowed action, discourage unproductive repeats, prefer a patch that addresses a defect and ask for relevant tests before finalization. The single-agent and independent adapters request JSON with \texttt{tool\allowbreak\_name} and \texttt{arguments}; the independent adapter assigns the actor by alternation. The coordinator also asks for \texttt{agent\allowbreak\_id}, so the model selects the actor. That is a procedural difference as well as a wording change.

The policy either parses a dictionary or extracts and decodes JSON from the response. It requires a string tool name and object arguments, checks any returned actor ID, and compares the tool and arguments with the current valid-action list. JSON formatting alone does not make an action valid; invented actions are rejected. The two nominal independent agents share the same state and action history, with role text and scheduled ID changing by turn. The coordinator is one decision process that may attribute an action to either actor.

The final independent-versus-prompted comparison is therefore between bundled policies, not prompt wording alone. Model settings, environment, tools and shared instructions are held constant, but role text and actor-selection authority change together. Statistical adjustment cannot recover a prompt-only effect when no condition holds assignment constant. That would require a matched or factorial experiment.

Retries occur at two levels. In \texttt{chat\allowbreak\_json()}, retryable connection, timeout, rate-limit and server errors are retried up to the configured limit, five by default, using exponential delay and jitter. Authentication, quota, access and other non-retryable errors are returned as request errors when soft fallback is disabled. At the policy level, malformed JSON, wrong response shapes, actions not on the valid list and unproductive repeats can trigger another request; two parse attempts are allowed by default. The retry prompt includes the validation error and asks again for one exact valid action. The audit records invalid responses, model errors and any fallback responses or locally selected actions.

In non-strict mode, a client setup or request failure can return a marked \texttt{FallbackResponse}. The policy records it and chooses a valid local action, preferring an update that was not just repeated. If both parse attempts fail, it also selects a local fallback. Neither is an unchanged model choice. In strict mode, a marked fallback raises \texttt{LLMFallbackError}, a model-call error raises \texttt{LLMCallError}, and an invalid response after retries raises \texttt{LLMResponseError}. The thesis run uses strict mode and saves the audit. Artifact validation rejects a strict summary that records a fallback response or action.

The runner treats strict fallbacks, exhausted API failures, authentication/access/quota errors and artifact/configuration failures as non-retryable collection failures. A final invalid model response is recorded as a retryable episode failure. Tests cover client formats, retry metadata, policy fallback and strict-mode rejection (\texttt{test\allowbreak\_llm\allowbreak\_client.py}, \texttt{test\allowbreak\_tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_baselines.py} and \texttt{test\allowbreak\_run\allowbreak\_baseline\allowbreak\_sweep.py}). These records separate recovery from the actions that eventually reach the environment.

\section{Manifest, assignment and resuming runs}
Before execution, the runner creates one manifest item for each policy, fixture and repetition. Each ID includes the policy, repetition, seed, task template and variant. For the thesis run, the builder pairs all 16 fixtures with four repetitions, assigns seeds starting at 200, and includes all three conditions. This produces 64 items per condition and 192 total. Each policy is assigned the same fixture, repetition and seed. In pilot mode, a seeded selection maps seeds to fixtures; in the thesis run, each fixture is named directly in the manifest.

The frozen \texttt{manifest.json} and \texttt{experiment\allowbreak\_config.json} preserve the planned assignments and settings. When resuming, the runner rebuilds the plan and stops if the manifest or comparable configuration fields have changed, except for creation time. \texttt{progress.json} records each item as \texttt{completed} or \texttt{failed}, with batch checkpoints and counts of completed, failed and pending items. On resume, completed IDs are skipped after their artifacts are found and checked. Failed IDs can be selected using the retry option. Another option lets the batch continue after an isolated retryable response failure; fatal errors stop it. The aggregate summary is recalculated from completed manifest entries and lists failures and pending items separately. An error/retry record describes an attempt, not an extra completed episode.

Before saving results or resuming, \texttt{\allowbreak\_validate\allowbreak\_completed\allowbreak\_artifact()} checks required files, summary version/outcome/identity, replay specification, JSONL header, event types and counts, fixture identity and strict-fallback counts. It also checks progress and error records against the manifest. These are structural checks, not checksums. The targeted audit in Section 9.6 checked identity, counts, event order and hash consistency; full schema, semantic and replay checks remain incomplete. Replay metadata does not make model responses deterministic. Tests cover manifest balance, fixture assignment, resume without duplicates, retry status and strict fallback (\texttt{test\allowbreak\_run\allowbreak\_baseline\allowbreak\_sweep.py}).

\section{Saved records and exports}
The function \texttt{persist\allowbreak\_episode\allowbreak\_run()} saves three main files per episode: \texttt{episode.jsonl}, \texttt{summary.json} and \texttt{task\allowbreak\_fixture.json}. The JSONL begins with an \texttt{episode\allowbreak\_header} holding schema and environment versions, configuration hash, seed, fixture, agent count, call budget and timestamp. Invalid calls are labelled \texttt{error\allowbreak\_or\allowbreak\_noop}. These tables are derived; the JSONL and episode summary remain the primary records.

\section{Coordination analysis and plots}
The script \texttt{coordination\allowbreak\_analysis.py} creates three representations for each agent: the full ordered action trace; counts for retrieval, verification, update, finalization and error/no-op, each capped at two; and a six-category \texttt{coarsened\allowbreak\_action\allowbreak\_profile}. The profile uses this priority: error/no-op, finalized, updated, verified, retrieved, then no actions. It loses action order and count details to reduce the number of possible categories. The report compares all three representations but uses the coarsened profile by default.

\textbf{Example of the profile rule.} Counts are ordered as \texttt{(retrieve, verify, update, finalize, error/no-op)} and capped at two. An agent who retrieves a file, patches it, tests and finalizes has counts \texttt{(1,1,1,1,0)}. An agent who only finalizes has \texttt{(0,0,0,1,0)}. Both become \texttt{finalized}; the first agent’s earlier actions disappear. In a joint profile, the position of \texttt{finalized} shows which actor ID was assigned the final action. The other actor is represented only by its highest-priority category. This compression therefore ties the measured profile directly to the stopping action.

The report calculates exploratory plug-in categorical mutual information, in bits, between the joint action profile \$(A\allowbreak\_0,A\allowbreak\_1)\$ and outcome \$Y\$ (\texttt{success}, \texttt{partial} or \texttt{failure}). This is empirical dependence between the encoded profiles and outcomes for eligible episodes. It is not a validated coordination measure, a temporal measure, a causal effect, a success rate or the paper’s time-delayed mutual information/PID synergy.

Because finalization has the highest profile priority, the estimate may mainly reflect which actor received the final action and the other actor’s highest remaining category. Two agents can have an associated joint profile even if they acted independently; the compression can also erase a real division of work. The estimate is therefore exploratory. Human-validated profiles, richer trace encodings and controlled coordination comparisons would be needed before interpreting it as coordination. Smoothing checks, bootstrap intervals and outcome-label permutations do not resolve these design limits. Estimator tests are in \texttt{test\allowbreak\_coordination\allowbreak\_metrics.py}; representation and eligibility tests are in \texttt{test\allowbreak\_coordination\allowbreak\_analysis.py}.

The estimate needs two observed agent traces, so the single-agent episodes are excluded. A separate one-agent calculation is reported only as a reference; it is not a two-agent coordination estimate. This is a structural difference, not missing data. Small samples, many joint categories and outcomes that rarely vary can all make the plug-in estimate unstable: sparse tables contain little information, and a constant outcome gives MI no outcome variation to explain. In the scripted pilot, full traces created many distinct categories while coarsening merged some of them. The final run also has only a few observed joint profiles and two outcome classes in each multi-agent condition. Coarsening makes estimation more manageable but does not remove its limitations. The all-success single-agent group cannot provide evidence about two-agent coordination, so no paired estimate is reported for it.

The script \texttt{results\allowbreak\_visualization.py} plots eligible MI and bootstrap intervals, invalid-call rate, mean calls, patch acceptance and recovery, plus test-call proportions and permutation p-values. The plots display saved statistics; they do not add a separate analysis. Remaining work in \texttt{validation.md} includes a full-data abstraction audit, human labelling, correlation analysis and deterministic scripted replay. A running pipeline does not, by itself, validate the meaning of its measures.

\section{Coordination estimates and their limits}
The saved \texttt{coordination\allowbreak\_report.json} calculates categorical mutual information between the two-agent action profile and episode outcome. The main representation, \texttt{coarsened\allowbreak\_action\allowbreak\_profile}, uses action counts capped at two and assigns each agent one of six labels. Priority goes to \texttt{error\allowbreak\_or\allowbreak\_noop}, then \texttt{finalized}, \texttt{updated}, \texttt{verified}, \texttt{retrieved} and \texttt{no\allowbreak\_actions}. This removes event order and count detail to reduce the number of categories (\texttt{coordination\allowbreak\_analysis.py}). The resulting statistic is an association in bits between encoded profiles and success, partial or failure. It is not a temporal or causal measure, nor the paper’s PID synergy.

For random policy, 17 of 20 episodes had events from two distinct agents and could be included in the paired estimate. The other three were excluded. Those 17 episodes contain eight joint profiles and three outcomes. Plug-in MI is 0.83545 bits; the saved 95\% bootstrap interval is approximately [0.3083, 1.3590], and the 250-permutation test gives \$p \textbackslash\{\}approx 0.1394\$. These results do not establish an association in a larger population or show that the profiles cause outcomes. With 17 episodes and eight categories, sparse cells and finite-sample bias are concerns.

Changing the representation changes the estimate. Full traces and capped action counts each produce 17 joint states and 1.37928 bits. The coarsened profile merges those into eight states and gives 0.83545 bits. The saved coordination artifact supports eight states; the validation tracker’s figures of nine states and 1.04 bits do not match this run. Coarsening reduces the state space, but does not prove that the profile labels capture coordination or remove estimation bias.

All 20 independent and 20 prompted-coordination episodes are eligible, but each condition has only one joint profile. Each has 16 successes and 4 partials, so outcomes are not constant; however, the profile never changes and no failures occur. MI is therefore 0 bits, with a zero-width bootstrap interval and permutation \$p=1\$. A constant profile cannot explain the outcome differences. Single-agent episodes are excluded from paired MI; a separate one-agent reference reports one profile and 0 bits across 20 episodes. It is not a two-agent estimate. The validation tracker’s note that three baselines have “constant outcomes” is inaccurate if taken literally. The issue here is that the two scripted multi-agent policies have constant joint profiles and limited outcome variation.

\section{What the pilot shows}
For these fixtures and policies, the runner completed 80 episodes, saved episode summaries and events, reconciled the outcomes, and exported files containing 546 events. The coordination pipeline was exercised on random policy, which varied in actions and outcomes, and on policies with repetitive profiles. This exposed how the chosen encoding and number of categories change the MI estimate. One number cannot, on its own, explain agent behaviour.

The results are limited. Each scripted condition has only 20 episodes; the task set is small and synthetic, and the budget is eight calls. Despite the configured model name, no LLM decisions were made. Three conditions follow the same seven-action pattern, which does not establish equivalence on other fixtures or seeds. Random-policy MI uses 17 eligible episodes with eight observed profiles. Fixture checks do not execute real programs. The run tested parts of the collection and analysis pipeline; it is not an external benchmark or a general ranking of multi-agent methods, and it does not test the paper’s claims about emergence, synergy, complementarity or goal-directed coordination. Conclusions should stay within these 80 episodes, policies and fixtures.

\section{Final run design}
This paired experiment was conducted after the pilots and calibration checks. Only the final run is analysed here; the earlier episodes are not pooled with it. The run used the \texttt{synthetic\allowbreak\_bugfix} environment and the Azure AI deployment recorded as \texttt{azureai/gpt-5-mini}. The three conditions were \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_single\allowbreak\_agent}, \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_independent\allowbreak\_multi\allowbreak\_agent} and \texttt{llm\allowbreak\_prompted\allowbreak\_coordination}. Each multi-agent episode used two agents; the single-agent policy used one. All episodes had a 12-call limit and strict LLM handling (\texttt{experiment\allowbreak\_config.json}).

The manifest pairs 16 fixture variants across the three conditions, with four repetitions per condition-fixture combination. That gives 64 episodes per condition and 192 altogether. Each comparison uses the same fixture/repetition identity and a shared seed; seeds start at 200. The fixtures cover arithmetic, strings, retry policies, flag parsing, billing, scheduling, ranking and inventory. They are synthetic: test calls check the fixture’s defect and regression state, not pytest, arbitrary source code or a real repository repair (\texttt{manifest.json}; \texttt{tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_environment.py}).

The manifest and summary files contain 192 unique IDs each, with one summary for every manifest item. Each condition has 64 summaries; each fixture variant has 12 items across conditions, one at each repetition from one to four. The final progress file records 192 completed, none failed or pending, and \texttt{run\allowbreak\_complete=true}. The aggregate summary agrees. These are completed episodes, not model-call or collection-attempt counts.

\section{Invalid responses and retries}
The policy audits record 96 recoverable invalid model responses across 81 completed episodes: 34 responses in 26 single-agent episodes, 35 in 30 independent multi-agent episodes and 27 in 25 prompted-coordination episodes. These were parsing or action-validation problems recovered within the policy loop; they were not invalid environment calls. The completed summaries record no fallback responses, no locally selected fallback actions and no model-call errors. Some model outputs therefore needed another attempt, but no soft-fallback action appears in the final traces (\texttt{thesis\allowbreak\_results\allowbreak\_qc.md}).

The run contains 56 episode-error records. All are retryable \texttt{LLMResponseError}s for 23 manifest IDs, and all 23 IDs appear among the final 192 summaries. Each record describes an attempt that still had an invalid response or action after two policy parse tries. In 42 records, the action did not match the current valid list; 13 recorded an action that should not be repeated; one had the wrong response types for \texttt{tool\allowbreak\_name} or \texttt{arguments}. Some IDs failed more than once before completing; one had nine error records.

\section{Saved artifacts and their limits}
The saved \texttt{thesis\allowbreak\_results\allowbreak\_qc.md} reports passing coverage, export, ordering, hash-consistency and strict-audit checks. A targeted audit confirmed that all 192 episodes have a JSONL header, summary and fixture; condition, seed, fixture and repetition match the manifest; and fixture IDs agree. Event counts match both \texttt{event\allowbreak\_count} and calls to completion. Event sequence IDs are contiguous, and header/event configuration hashes match the summaries. The coordination collector returned no malformed episodes, and all 1,181 events reconcile with summary counts. These checks cover identity, counts, order and hash consistency; they are not a full schema, semantic or replay audit.

The export manifest lists 192 episodes, 1,181 event rows and no export issues. The CSV files contain 1,181 event records and 192 summaries. Both Parquet files were opened separately and have the same row counts. The saved PDFs, \texttt{plots/coordination\allowbreak\_summary.pdf} and \texttt{plots/tool\allowbreak\_use\allowbreak\_dashboard.pdf}, display MI estimates and intervals alongside action, reliability, call, patch, recovery and test-call summaries. They plot existing measures; they do not add a new inferential analysis.

These checks show that files agree internally and can be read; they cannot rule out every kind of corruption. Runner checks are not cryptographic checksums, and no independent review of every fixture judgement was performed. The environment’s tests inspect simulated defect state; they do not execute exported or patched code. The experiment also uses one model/provider, three policy implementations and one synthetic task catalogue. Fixed manifest assignments do not make model service state or response sampling deterministic. Phase 2 is not a direct performance comparison with Phase 1 and does not reproduce the paper’s guessing task or information-theoretic analyses.

Manifest, retry and audit handling improved during later maintenance. Those changes do not establish which safeguards were in place when earlier files were produced. The saved configuration and artifacts remain the evidence for the results in this chapter and allow them to be checked again.

\section{What the final run shows}
The final collection is balanced across 16 fixtures and three conditions, with consistent episode records and tabular exports. All 64 single-agent episodes succeeded. Each multi-agent condition had partials but no failures. The environment accepted all patch calls and recorded no invalid tool calls. Those results reflect selection from a filtered action list that includes exact catalogue patch text; they do not show unrestricted code generation. The strict audit has no final fallback or model-call error, though recoverable invalid responses and failed collection attempts are recorded in the audit trail.

The result supports a narrow conclusion: this configured model completed these synthetic tasks at the recorded rates and produced the saved traces. Outcome differences, call counts, workload, fixture comparisons and action-profile MI are descriptive. MI is especially tentative: the profiles prioritise finalization, outcomes are sparse, fixture difficulty may affect both profile and outcome, and six coordinator episodes have a distinct no-action profile. Single-agent episodes cannot provide a paired two-agent estimate. The dataset is auditable, but does not show that multi-agent policies outperform the single-agent policy, that one instruction is better, or that paper-style emergence or synergy occurred.

\section{How the phases fit together}
The project has two experimental phases and a later bridge. Phase 1 adapts the repeated group-guessing task in \emph{Emergent Coordination in Multi-Agent Language Models}. Phase 2 grew from a question about measurement: could the agents’ operations, the changing task state and the final outcome be recorded more directly? A synthetic bug-fix task and explicit tool actions were used to explore that question. After the Phase 2 runs, the bridge returned to the guessing game and recorded each contribution as an action. The work developed in stages, but the tasks, observations and analyses differ.

The connection is methodological, not a comparison of performance. Phase 1 adapts a shared-feedback guessing task; Phase 2 records actions and state in a different synthetic task. The later bridge checks selected guessing-game transitions against saved guesses. None of these results demonstrates the paper’s emergence or synergy findings. Those terms depend on temporal and information-decomposition analyses, not simply task success, varied actions or categorical dependence (Riedl, 2026, pp. 2–6; Chapters \hyperref[chapter-01]{1}, \hyperref[chapter-03]{3} and \hyperref[chapter-05]{5}).

The project questions in Section 1.4 compare episode outcomes, action volume and tool mix, agent participation, and stopping behaviour. The final run pairs fixtures and repetitions across three policies, but uses one model and has near-ceiling success rates. The questions can therefore be answered descriptively, not as causal or broadly generalisable claims.

\section{What Phase 1 kept from the paper}
In the paper’s task, agents guess privately toward a hidden target and receive shared “too high” or “too low” feedback about the group result. Phase 1 keeps this basic pattern: agents guess, the engine combines their values and everyone receives the same directional feedback over repeated rounds. Each prompt includes that agent’s earlier guesses, not the guesses of its peers. This is a limited adaptation of the paper’s group-guessing task (Riedl, 2026, p. 3; Chapter \hyperref[chapter-02]{2}).

The maintained implementation has sum and mean modes, configurable model and round settings, a persona branch, and per-run prompts, logs, round records, responses and summaries. Parsing, fallbacks, API compatibility and result extraction were revised over time. The one saved local example is an unsolved 20-round sum game; it is not a completed sweep or a population-level result (saved configuration, summary and log).

This is not a full reproduction. The repository uses its own target and guess ranges, optional mean mode, prompts, model handling and run design. The persona path combines persona text with instructions about other agents, and there are no matched Plain, Persona-only and Persona-plus-ToM conditions. Phase 1 also lacks the paper’s time-delayed mutual information, PID, macro-signal tests, differentiation models, coalition tests and related null analyses. It adapts the task idea, not the paper’s full protocol, experiment or analysis. The saved Phase 1 data cannot support a new claim about emergence or synergy.

\section{What Phase 2 added}
The framework changed in stages. Phase 1 records numerical guesses and shared directional feedback. The later wrapper represents each guess as \texttt{submit\allowbreak\_guess(value)} with an actor ID, then replays saved values against the original round rules. Phase 2 is a larger change: 16 synthetic fixtures have explicit intermediate state and tools for retrieval, updates, verification and finalization. The five tools, valid-action rules, state changes and success/partial/failure outcomes can all be inspected. The environment checks arguments, references, state and call budget, and classifies invalid actions. Its deterministic evaluator checks predefined defects and regression conditions; it does not run real tests or arbitrary patched code (Phase 1 wrapper, replay runner and Phase 2 environment; Chapter \hyperref[chapter-05]{5}).

The project’s methodological contribution is the added action representation, visible task state and saved action-to-outcome trace. In this setting, participation, differences in action use and verification before stopping can be studied as possible coordination signals. The bridge checks the wrapper and task transitions; it does not measure those behaviours or show that they improve coordination.

The protocols remain different. Phase 1 agents use private guess histories and receive common feedback after each round. Phase 2 policies take turns in shared context and attach nominal actor IDs to actions. The bridge does not make these protocols equivalent or establish autonomous collaboration. Phase 2 records fixtures, calls, arguments, outcomes, invalid actions and progress. Manifests separate planned from completed episodes; strict audits keep response recovery records apart from final summaries. Exports support descriptive analysis. Model choices are limited to a changing list of tools and catalogue patches, including visible replacement text. The experiment measures action selection and completion under these constraints, not unrestricted code repair. Saved assignments and traces help inspect the run but do not make model responses deterministic.

Phase 2 checks each episode against fixture rules instead of inferring progress from a shared signal. Here, “success” means that the required fixture defects are marked fixed and the fixture checks pass; it does not mean real software was repaired. The action-profile MI remains exploratory. It measures dependence between paired action categories and outcomes, not the paper’s time-lagged decomposition. It does not show causation, synergy, differentiation or complementarity. The Phase 1 bridge shows that actions can be represented and audited in a related way, not that the experiments are equivalent. Neither the bridge nor Phase 2 establishes emergence, autonomous collaboration or improved coordination.

\section{Findings from the saved runs}
The 80-episode scripted pilot shows that four scripted policies ran through the environment and produced summaries, coordination output and exports. It also exposed issues with representation and sample size: random-policy MI used 17 eligible episodes, while the multi-agent profiles in the other scripted conditions did not vary. The artifacts contain 546 events; the higher count in the validation note is stale (Chapter \hyperref[chapter-07]{7}).

The 11 September LLM pilots and 12 September calibration runs are separate from the final study. Each of the three early pilots has three episodes on one fixture; they recorded failures, partials and successes at different budgets, but cannot isolate a call-budget effect. Two calibration attempts stopped before an episode completed. The preflight probe and thesis calibration each completed three episodes. A 192-item manifest does not turn unrun items into observations (Chapter \hyperref[chapter-08]{8}).

The final run is the largest saved Phase 2 dataset: 192 completed episodes, balanced across three conditions and 16 engineered fixtures, with four repetitions per condition/fixture combination. Success was 64/64 for single-agent (100.00\%; 95\% Wilson CI: 94.34–100.00\%), 60/64 for independent multi-agent (93.75\%; 85.00–97.54\%), and 63/64 for prompted coordination (98.44\%; 91.67–99.72\%). Overall, 187 episodes succeeded and five were partial; none failed. Paired success-rate differences were +6.25 percentage points for single-agent versus independent multi-agent (95\% fixture-cluster interval: 0.00 to +15.62), +1.56 for single-agent versus prompted coordination (0.00 to +4.69), and +4.69 for prompted versus independent multi-agent (0.00 to +12.50). These describe one model/provider and a synthetic task set; they do not establish superiority, equivalence or causation. The environment accepted all 403 patch calls and recorded no invalid submitted calls (Chapter \hyperref[chapter-09]{9}).

An offline analysis of the same episodes found greater average workload imbalance under prompted coordination (33.08\%) than independent multi-agent (6.81\%). Tool-mix TVD was also higher when both agents acted (0.884 versus 0.480). These are descriptive differences that may reflect the actor-selection rules; they do not show emergent specialisation. All five partials were in billing (19/24 successes); the other seven families had 168/168 successes (Section 9.7).

All five partial episodes occurred on billing fixtures and ended with \texttt{finalize\allowbreak\_ticket} while a required defect remained, despite calls left in the budget. The matching single-agent fixture/repetition episodes succeeded. This shows early stopping in these traces, not why it happened or whether multi-agent coordination caused it (Section 9.3).

The strict audit records 96 recoverable invalid responses across 81 episodes, with no final fallback actions or model-call errors. A further 56 retryable error records concern 23 manifest IDs, all of which later completed. These are failed attempts, not additional episodes. A completed run does not mean every first response was valid or that no retries occurred.

Categorical MI is about 0.0466 bits for independent multi-agent and 0.0400 bits for prompted coordination, using 64 and 58 eligible episodes. The coordinator estimate excludes six episodes without events from both agents. Outcome entropy is 0.3373 and 0.1257 bits, respectively, reflecting the few partial outcomes. The independent-policy bootstrap interval excludes zero, but the outcome-shuffle permutation test is non-significant (\(p=0.207\)). The bootstrap describes resampling variation around a non-negative estimate; the permutation test compares it with a shuffled no-association null. They answer different questions. Neither compares policies or establishes a coordination mechanism. Single-agent episodes cannot be included in paired MI. These estimates describe dependence under this encoding, not paper-style emergence or synergy.

\section{Limits and unfinished checks}
Phase 2 uses a finite set of engineered fixtures. Checks use an in-memory defect catalogue, not real software execution or review. Completion rates therefore describe performance under this task contract, not general software-engineering ability. The final dataset also uses one model/provider, so it cannot show that results generalise across models or settings.

The three conditions are not the paper’s Plain, Persona and ToM design. The multi-agent conditions change both role instructions and actor-selection rules, so their contrast cannot isolate either factor. The single-agent condition is a task reference, not a two-agent coordination condition. Phase 1 and Phase 2 also use different tasks, actions, outcomes and samples; their success rates are not directly comparable.

The categorical profile removes action order and count. It has not been validated through human labelling or inter-rater agreement. With 64 episodes per condition, few partials and few joint profiles, MI remains exploratory. Its bootstrap and label-permutation procedures do not preserve fixture clusters and do not establish causality or policy differences. Retries are also separate from the 192 completed episodes: the 56 failed-attempt records for 23 IDs and the recovered invalid responses are not environment-invalid tool calls.

The completed checks support internal consistency, not a full semantic audit. Human review, metric correlations, deterministic Phase 2 replay, more models, real-task validation and the paper’s temporal PID/synergy analyses remain future work. Persona studies were not included in Phase 2. The Phase 1 bridge only replays saved guesses; it does not close these gaps.

\section{Future work}
Several next steps follow directly from these limits:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Test more tasks.} Apply the action/outcome framework to more fixture families, then to isolated real repositories with executable tests and human review. Keep fixture success separate from externally checked repair quality.
\item \textbf{Use more models.} Repeat the balanced manifest with other models and providers. Save version and sampling details, and run enough independent samples to distinguish model, fixture and condition variation.
\item \textbf{Separate condition effects.} Pre-register matched comparisons that vary coordinator instructions, agent count and turn allocation independently. Retain a one-agent reference without treating it as a two-agent observation.
\item \textbf{Check action labels.} Ask independent reviewers to label a stratified set of traces, resolve disagreements and report agreement and category coverage. Compare the coarse profile with richer sequence encodings using criteria set in advance.
\item \textbf{Improve replay and provenance.} Replay scripted episodes from saved fixtures and seeds, check state/event equivalence, and record the code, configuration and environment used. Treat LLM replay separately because model responses can vary.
\item \textbf{Strengthen measurement.} Choose sample sizes and comparisons that allow outcomes to vary; report uncertainty and pre-specify tests. If emergence is a future aim, implement and validate temporal information measures and null tests as a separate study, rather than relabelling categorical MI.
\item \textbf{Study retry effects.} Report first-response validity, recovery rates, attempts per completed ID and any link between retries and trajectories. Keep technical failures out of episode denominators while reporting the collection effort.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Conclusion}
The project moved from group guessing with shared feedback to a synthetic tool-use study, then added a bridge back to the guessing task. Phase 1 keeps a recognisable part of the paper’s task, but not its full protocol, treatment design or information-theoretic analysis. Phase 2 records actions, state changes and outcomes for a different task. Its final run contains 192 completed synthetic episodes with a balanced design, saved retry history and descriptive categorical estimates.

The final run answers the project questions as follows, within the limits of this study:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Project RQ1 --- Completion:} Single-agent succeeded in 64/64 episodes (100.00\%; 95\% Wilson CI: 94.34--100.00\%). Independent multi-agent succeeded in 60/64 (93.75\%; 85.00--97.54\%) and had four partials. Prompted coordination succeeded in 63/64 (98.44\%; 91.67--99.72\%) and had one partial. Paired differences ranged from +1.56 to +6.25 percentage points; all fixture-cluster intervals include zero. There were no failures. These are descriptive differences, not evidence of superiority, equivalence or causation.
\item \textbf{Project RQ2 --- Action use:} The policies recorded 416, 394 and 371 calls, with mean calls per episode of 6.500, 6.156 and 5.797. All 403 patch calls were accepted, and no submitted tool call was invalid. Prompted coordination had higher workload imbalance and tool-mix TVD than independent multi-agent. These differences may reflect actor-assignment rules; they are not evidence of emergent specialisation.
\item \textbf{Project RQ3 --- Stopping behaviour:} Each condition recorded 64 finalizations. Mean calls through finalization were 6.50 for single-agent, 6.16 for independent multi-agent and 5.80 for prompted coordination. Premature finalization occurred in 0/64, 4/64 (6.25\%) and 1/64 (1.56\%) episodes, respectively. The five partials stopped after four to six calls, before the 12-call limit, with billing defects unresolved. The traces identify premature stopping as the immediate observed issue, but do not reveal why the model stopped.
\end{enumerate}
The project produced a synthetic environment, policy adapters, a collection/export pipeline, the 192-episode dataset, descriptive findings and the later Phase 1 bridge. These are separate from the inherited guessing-game implementation and the paper’s concepts; they are not claims of field-wide novelty. The contribution is methodological: the tool-use extension makes one synthetic workflow easier to inspect and check under fixed rules.

The model selected from supplied, state-filtered actions, including catalogue patches with visible replacement text. It did not generate unrestricted repairs. The zero invalid-call rate and 100\% patch acceptance mainly reflect that constrained interface. Action-profile MI remains exploratory: the priority rule compresses behavior into stopping/actor labels, and fixture family may affect both profiles and outcomes. The bridge checks environment transitions for supplied, already-parsed guesses; it does not show emergence, PID synergy, autonomous collaboration, improved coordination or equivalence with Phase 2.

Participation, action differences and verification before stopping may be useful signals for future study. This experiment does not show that multi-agent policies outperform a single agent, that one coordination instruction is better, or that the paper’s emergence and synergy findings carry over to tool-use tasks. Those claims would require broader task and model coverage, validated measures and a design built to test them.
